TITLE: Compressed Schedules, Fatigue and Workload Intensity
SOURCE TYPE: Synthetic test paper (fictional data, for testing only)
TOPIC: Effects of a four-day work week on productivity and wellbeing

1. TWO DIFFERENT MODELS

Not every four-day week is the same. In a reduced-hours model, staff work about
32 hours over four days. In a compressed model, staff work the same 40 hours in
four days of 10 hours each. This review compared 12 studies, 7 of the
reduced-hours model and 5 of the compressed model.

2. FATIGUE IN COMPRESSED SCHEDULES

Studies of the compressed model found that 10-hour days increased end-of-day
fatigue. In two studies of manufacturing workers, error rates rose in the last
two hours of a 10-hour shift. Some employees liked having a third day off, but
others found the longer days hard to manage alongside childcare.

3. WORKLOAD INTENSITY IN REDUCED-HOURS SCHEDULES

In the reduced-hours studies, wellbeing usually improved. However, 4 of the 7
studies reported that work became more intense: employees had fewer breaks and
felt more pressure to finish tasks within four days. In one study, 30 percent of
employees said they regularly worked on their day off to keep up.

4. CONCLUSIONS

The review concludes that the benefits of a four-day week depend strongly on
which model is used and on whether the workload is actually reduced. Reduced
hours with a reorganised workload gave the most consistent wellbeing gains.
Compressed schedules showed mixed results and some safety concerns in physical
jobs. The authors warn that most studies were short and relied on
self-reported data, and they call for longer trials with objective measures.
