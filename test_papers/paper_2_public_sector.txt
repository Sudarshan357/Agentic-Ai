TITLE: Reduced Working Hours in Public Sector Services
SOURCE TYPE: Synthetic test paper (fictional data, for testing only)
TOPIC: Effects of a four-day work week on productivity and wellbeing

1. BACKGROUND

Between 2015 and 2019, a city government ran a trial in which about 2,500
public employees reduced their working week from 40 hours to 35 or 36 hours
without a cut in pay. Participating workplaces included offices, preschools,
social service centres and a hospital ward.

2. SERVICE OUTPUT

In office-based workplaces, service output stayed the same or improved slightly.
Managers reorganised meetings and shortened routine tasks to fit the shorter
week.

In workplaces with fixed staffing needs, such as the preschools and the hospital
ward, the results were different. These sites could not keep the same opening
hours without hiring extra staff. Overall, the city hired the equivalent of 40
additional full-time employees to cover the gaps, which raised staffing costs.

3. EMPLOYEE WELLBEING

Workers reported less stress and better work-life balance. Reported stress
symptoms fell in most workplaces, and employees said they had more time for
family and household tasks. The study found no clear change in sick leave in the
hospital ward.

4. CONCLUSIONS AND CAVEATS

The authors conclude that shorter hours can work in office settings without a
loss of output. They stress that shift-based services usually need extra hiring,
so the policy is not cost-free in those sectors. The trial reduced hours by
about 4 to 5 hours a week, which is less than a full four-day week, so the
results may not apply directly to a 32-hour schedule.
