TITLE: Four-Day Work Week Trial and Employee Productivity
SOURCE TYPE: Synthetic test paper (fictional data, for testing only)
TOPIC: Effects of a four-day work week on productivity and wellbeing

1. STUDY DESIGN

This trial followed 61 companies that moved from a five-day week to a four-day
week for six months. Employees kept 100 percent of their pay while working about
80 percent of their previous hours. Most participating companies were small
office-based firms in marketing, software and professional services. Only 4 of
the 61 companies worked in manufacturing or retail.

Productivity was measured with each company's own metrics, such as revenue per
employee or tickets resolved. Wellbeing was measured with a staff survey at the
start and end of the trial.

2. PRODUCTIVITY RESULTS

Average revenue across the participating companies rose by 1.4 percent during
the trial compared with the same period a year earlier. The authors note that
this change is small and is not statistically significant. Their conclusion is
that productivity was maintained, not that it increased.

At the end of the trial, 56 of the 61 companies chose to continue the four-day
week, and 18 made it permanent.

3. WELLBEING RESULTS

Self-reported burnout fell: 71 percent of employees reported lower levels of
burnout at the end of the trial. Sick days fell by about 65 percent compared
with the year before. Staff turnover dropped by 57 percent over the trial
period.

4. LIMITATIONS

The companies volunteered for the trial, so they were likely already open to
flexible working and may not represent typical employers. The trial lasted only
six months, so the long-term effects are unknown. Wellbeing results are based on
self-reported surveys, which can be influenced by employees' enthusiasm for the
policy. The authors state that the results should not be generalised to sectors
that need staff present at fixed hours, such as healthcare or retail.
